# Text and annotation decisions — version 7

This is a plain, unlinked maintenance record. It is not an editorial section of the reading site. It contains later-volume names and occasional passage details, so it is not spoiler-free. The reading popups avoid explanations of plot events and character identities.

## Scope

The reading text and chapter addresses are based on the delivered version 6. This pass accepts the focused typography proposals where they improve reading, restores useful annotations previously withheld for ambiguity, and slightly widens the literary-vocabulary threshold. It does not run a blanket spellchecker replacement over invented names, dialect, or old spellings.

An ordinary dictionary can establish what a word means without establishing why Gene Wolfe selected it. A saint’s life establishes the saint’s tradition, not an author-confirmed character identification. Where the connection is tentative, the definition says “Possibly.” Documented linguistic meanings are distinguished from proposed namesakes. Legends are described as legends.

Lexicon Urthus and Gate of Horn, Book of Silk were used as checking guides and research leads. Neither is reproduced here or used as a popup source. Independently linked dictionaries, museum and library pages, historical texts, and scholarly references support the external information in the revised notes. A few transparent contextual words are explained as such; an unrelated dictionary page is not supplied to disguise a missing outside source.

This is not a full collation with a publisher’s edition, not exhaustive annotation of every unusual expression, and not a new independent verification of every note inherited from version 6. The links listed below identify the actual evidence used for this pass; they do not certify authorial intent.

### Resolved by retaining the source

**Yrierix — retained.** The old query proposed Yrieix, but Yrierix is a transmitted spelling, and an extra R may predate Wolfe. A doubtful historical-name normalization does not improve this sentence enough to justify changing it. The typography question is closed as “keep,” not left pending. The earlier reference work’s published errata are relevant to the internal decision, not a popup citation.

**“winter, the” in Citadel VII, paragraph 029 — retained.** The previous case-insensitive audit mistakenly grouped this phrase with the capitalized “winter, The” sentence boundary in Shadow I. In Citadel the comma belongs to a legitimate continuing phrase about winter, storms, and freezing fogs. Only the capitalized Shadow occurrence was changed. This is why raw flag counts were not treated as error counts.

**“fo-reign” in On Blue’s Waters II — retained as hesitant speech.** Gate of Horn’s typo appendix itself leaves open an intentional delivery. It need not be smoothed into an ordinary word.

**“lay down” in the Hoof-written section of Return to the Whorl XVII — retained as voice.** The guide explicitly allows this usage as the narrator’s language.

**The possible “Thank you” quotation/paragraph adjustment in Return to the Whorl IV — retained.** The guide offers it tentatively in the delirium sequence; the supplied punctuation can be read coherently. It does not justify restructuring the paragraph in this pass.

**Hyphenation families, repeated unusual names, and the old broad spelling/punctuation screens — not bulk-replaced.** Those inventories were candidate generators, not an approved correction list. They contain valid forms, authorial speech, and false positives. No unresolved audit page or checklist is presented to the reader.

The focused earlier list had 23 query types. Twenty-two receive a normalization or repair; Yrierix receives a keep decision. Multiple occurrences account for 25 replacement spans from that focused list. Two further clear transcription errors in the opening of Long Sun are also repaired: “apparent He” and “catch ft.” The erroneous second “winter, The” match is separately dismissed above.

### Important distinctions

**Planteration** is restored as compulsory eating used as torment, not as transplantation of vegetation. No plausible-looking “plantation” link is used. The note is a contextual gloss, so there is no external-source button for this particular word.

**Exultant** names a rank in the narrative. Its dictionary link supports the ordinary adjective and its history, not the existence of that fictional rank. **Vodalarius** similarly has a transparent contextual affiliation; the external evidence concerns the Latin-like suffix.

**Arctother** is qualified as a possible giant short-faced bear. The museum reference describes Arctodus simus, but does not identify a fictional animal as an exact modern genus or species.

**Severian, Malrubius, Cyriaca, Maxellindis, Nicarete, Palaemon, Roche, and Fechin** present proposed historical namesakes. **Agia** and **Valeria** give the language background without pretending that one of several saints must be intended.

**Terminus Est, Acies, and Capulus** admit related Latin senses. Their notes explain the range instead of choosing an overly precise single translation.

**Jahlee** is possibly related to Hindi jālī, “counterfeit”; the similarly romanized net/lattice word has a distinct history. **Chandi** is possibly the word for silver; a deity with a similar Latin-letter spelling is not silently substituted.

**Canna** is restricted to the Flier-device passage in Exodus XII. Elsewhere the same spelling can be the ordinary contraction “cannot.” **Mora** receives the Italian-fruit note only in Short Sun, not for the different New Sun usage.

**Viron** is qualified with its Old French circle/surroundings sense. **Trivigaunte** is qualified with Termagant, explicitly identified as a medieval Christian literary misrepresentation rather than an actual Islamic deity. **Alambrera** uses the Spanish wire-screen sense, not “prison” as an ordinary dictionary meaning.

**Hari Mau** uses the independently attested Malay word harimau, tiger, as its linguistic background. The guide’s alternative Hindi wordplay is not asserted as a translation. **Bricco** receives the small pouring-vessel sense, rather than the guide’s questionable “brick” translation.

Annotation scope now includes ordinary-looking personal names, animal products and anatomy, botanical and material names, geographical names, civic/religious titles, historical demonyms, fictional vocabulary, and less-common literary words. Capitalization alone does not establish a namesake. Contextual meanings and dictionary meanings are distinguished; possible derivations remain explicitly tentative.
This is a focused new source review and a complete structural audit, not a claim that all earlier historical identifications have been independently proved. Sources were checked for the new notes and revised claims; existing Siren-name attributions were also checked against the classical passages collected by Theoi and retained. No book wording or paragraph addresses were changed.
Corrections: azoth now identifies the fictional weapon separately from the alchemical word; talus separates the machine from its mythological comparison; Patera and Maytera distinguish fictional titles from Greek comparisons; Azijin explicitly marks the spelling difference from Dutch azijn; Chenille includes its textile sense; Baldanders cites Wolfe’s own account of borrowing from Borges.
